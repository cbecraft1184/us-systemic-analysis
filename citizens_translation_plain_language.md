# Why Everything Feels Harder Than It Should: A Plain-Language Guide to What Changed in America

**By a regular citizen, using research from economists, sociologists, political scientists, and historians.**

**Reading time: ~20 minutes**

---

## Introduction: The Promise and the Receipt

You were told that hard work, a steady job, and responsible choices would build a stable life. You held up your end of the deal. You showed up, paid your dues, and did what was asked.

But somewhere between your effort and your outcome, the rules changed without telling you.

If you have ever sat down with your bills, your paycheck, and a calculator, and felt a quiet panic that the numbers no longer add up the way you were told they would—this is for you. If you have ever wondered why a single medical bill, a rent increase, or a layoff feels like it could unravel everything you have built—this explains it. If you have looked at your parents' life, your neighbors' life, or your own future and thought, *"I am doing everything right. Why does it feel like I am falling behind?"*—you are not alone, and you are not imagining it.

It is not laziness. It is not avocado toast or streaming subscriptions or a failure of character.

The American economy underwent a quiet but total regime shift around 1973. Since then, the system has been redesigned—slowly, legally, and invisibly—to move wealth upward and leave ordinary families holding the bill. This document explains what changed, why it reaches into your daily life, and what we can realistically do about it.

You do not need to be an expert to understand this. You just need to be someone who pays rent, buys groceries, and wonders why the math stopped working.

## Part 1: The Big Shift—What Changed Around 1973

### The Deal Your Parents Got

From roughly 1945 to 1973, the United States had an unwritten deal: if the economy grew, your life got better.

When factories got more efficient, workers got raises. When companies made more money, they shared it. One income could buy a house, raise kids, and fund a retirement. A high school graduate could support a family. College was cheap or free at public universities. If you got sick, you could pay the bill without mortgaging your future.

This was not magic. It was policy. Strong unions gave workers bargaining power. Banks were regulated so they could not gamble with the economy. The government built highways, schools, and research labs with public money. Taxes on the very rich were high—up to 91% at the top bracket—which kept wealth from concentrating at the top and funded the investments that made everyone richer.

### The Deal Broke

Around 1973, that deal started to unravel. Here is what happened.

**1. Wages Stopped Rising with Productivity**

"Productivity" means how much value the economy creates per hour of work. From 1945 to 1973, as productivity went up, wages went up at the same rate. After 1973, productivity kept climbing—but wages flatlined.

Where did the extra money go? To shareholders, executives, and financial institutions. The economy kept growing, but the growth was siphoned upward before it reached workers.

**What this means for you:** The economy is bigger than ever, but your paycheck does not reflect it. You are producing more value than previous generations did at your age, but you are not being paid for it.

**2. Unions Were Dismantled**

In the 1950s, about 35% of American workers belonged to a union. Today, it is about 10%. Unions were not perfect, but they did one thing extremely well: they made sure that when companies made money, workers got a share.

Without unions, the bargaining table became one-sided. Employers could set wages without counterpressure. The result was predictable: corporate profits and CEO pay skyrocketed, while median wages stagnated.

**What this means for you:** You are negotiating your salary alone, against a corporation with an HR department, lawyers, and a board of directors. That is not a negotiation. That is a receipt.

**3. Finance Took Over the Economy**

In 1970, the financial sector (banks, investment firms, insurance companies) made up about 15% of the economy. Today, it is about 20%. That may not sound like much, but it represents trillions of dollars that used to go into building things—factories, infrastructure, research—and now go into moving money around.

Wall Street learned that it could make more profit from stock buybacks, derivatives, and real estate speculation than from actually making products or paying workers.

**What this means for you:** Capital that could have built your community or raised your wages is instead being used to inflate asset prices and enrich shareholders.

**4. Housing Became a Wealth Machine Instead of a Home**

Previous generations probably bought a house for about 3–4 years of their income. Today, in most parts of the country, it takes 7–8 years of median income to buy a home. In some cities, it is 10–15 years.

This did not happen because houses got that much better. It happened because housing was turned into an investment asset. Hedge funds, private equity, and real estate investment trusts started buying homes by the thousands. Zoning laws were written to restrict supply. The result: existing homeowners got richer, and everyone else got priced out.

**What this means for you:** Your rent is high because someone else's portfolio needs to appreciate. The system is designed so that housing gets more expensive, because expensive housing makes asset-holders wealthier.

**5. Healthcare Became a Profit Center**

The United States spends about 17–18% of its total economy on healthcare. That is roughly double what other wealthy countries spend. And yet Americans have shorter life expectancies, higher infant mortality, and more medical debt than citizens of countries that spend half as much.

Where does the extra money go? Administrative bloat (billing departments, insurance middlemen), pharmaceutical pricing, and defensive medicine driven by fear of lawsuits. The system is not designed to keep you healthy. It is designed to bill you.

**What this means for you:** Even if you have insurance, a single medical emergency can wipe out your savings. Medical debt is the leading cause of personal bankruptcy in America. You are one diagnosis away from financial ruin.

**6. College Became a Debt Trap**

Public universities used to be heavily subsidized by state governments. Starting in the 1980s, states cut funding and shifted costs to students. Tuition rose far faster than inflation. To pay for it, students took out loans.

Today, student loan debt in the U.S. exceeds $1.7 trillion. It is larger than credit card debt and auto loan debt combined.

**What this means for you:** Young people graduate with a mortgage-sized debt burden before they ever buy a house. This delays marriage, childbearing, homeownership, and entrepreneurship. It is a drag on the entire economy.

## Part 2: The Human Cost—How This Shows Up in Your Life

### The Coin Flip

Here is a statistic that should stop you in your tracks.

If you were born in 1940, you had a 90% chance of earning more than your parents did.

If you were born in 1980, you had a 50% chance.

We did not get lazier. The system stopped sharing the returns.

### The Geography of Inequality

Economic opportunity is no longer spread across the country. It is concentrated in a handful of coastal cities: New York, Boston, Washington, San Francisco, Seattle, Los Angeles. If you do not live in one of these places—or cannot afford to move there—your economic prospects are structurally worse.

Meanwhile, rural areas and former manufacturing towns have been hollowed out. The jobs left. The young people left. The tax base collapsed. And the people who remained were told their economic pain was their own fault.

### Deaths of Despair

In the last two decades, something unprecedented happened in the United States: death rates rose for a large group of working-age Americans. Not from disease. From suicide, drug overdose, and alcohol-related liver failure.

This is almost entirely concentrated among Americans without a college degree. The causes are not mysterious: the collapse of stable employment, the erosion of community institutions (churches, unions, clubs), and the flood of potent opioids into communities that had lost hope.

No other wealthy country has this pattern. It is uniquely American. And it is a direct result of economic and social structures that stopped working for ordinary people.

### The Trust Collapse

In the 1960s, most Americans trusted the government, the media, corporations, and each other. Today, trust in nearly every major institution has collapsed.

This is not because people became cynical. It is because institutions repeatedly failed to deliver. When you watch banks get bailed out while your neighborhood decays, when you see politicians argue while your bills rise, when you read about record corporate profits while your wages stall—you stop believing the system works for you.

Low trust is not just a feeling. It is a structural problem. It makes it harder to solve collective problems, because nobody believes anyone else is acting in good faith.

## Part 3: The Political Gridlock—Why Nothing Seems to Get Fixed

### The System Was Built for Compromise

The American political system was designed in the 18th century for a world where political parties were not very different from each other. It assumes that people will disagree, then make a deal.

That system breaks down when the two sides stop trusting each other entirely. And that is where we are.

### Why Gridlock Happens

**1. The Senate and Electoral College Favor Geography Over People**

Wyoming has 580,000 people and gets two senators. California has 39 million people and gets two senators. This means that a voter in a small, rural state has roughly 70 times more Senate representation than a voter in a large state.

The Electoral College works the same way. It is possible to win the presidency while losing the national popular vote, because the system weights land more than people.

This is not a partisan statement. It is a mathematical fact. And it means that the preferences of the majority of Americans are often overridden by the preferences of a geographic minority.

**2. Primary Elections Reward Extremism**

In most congressional districts, the general election is not competitive. The district is safely Democratic or safely Republican. That means the real election is the primary, where only the most motivated voters show up.

Primary voters tend to be more ideological than general election voters. So politicians who want to keep their jobs have an incentive to appeal to the extremes, not the center. The result is a Congress full of people who cannot compromise because their voters will punish them for it.

**3. Money in Politics**

Running for Congress costs millions of dollars. Running for president costs billions. To raise that money, candidates depend on wealthy donors, corporations, and PACs. This does not mean politicians are corrupt. It means they must spend enormous amounts of time listening to people who can write big checks, rather than people who cannot.

**4. The Media Ecosystem**

Forty years ago, most Americans got their news from the same three TV networks. Today, news is fragmented across cable channels, websites, and social media algorithms that show you what you already believe.

This creates "filter bubbles" where people on the left and right do not just disagree on policy—they live in different realities. And when you believe the other side is not just wrong but evil, compromise becomes impossible.

### The New Judicial Veto

For decades, when Congress could not act, presidents used federal agencies to write rules: the EPA for environment, the FTC for competition, the FDA for drugs. Courts generally deferred to agency expertise.

In 2024, the Supreme Court ended that deference. Now, a single federal judge can strike down an agency rule. This means that even when an administration tries to act without Congress, the judiciary can block it.

The result is a three-branch standoff: Congress is gridlocked, the President is constrained, and the Courts are active. Nothing moves.

## Part 4: The AI Shock—The Next Wave Is Already Here

There is one more change coming, and it is happening faster than most people realize.

Artificial intelligence is not just coming for factory jobs. It is coming for white-collar work: analysts, paralegals, programmers, accountants, administrators, writers, and designers.

This is different from the automation of the 20th century, which mostly affected manual labor. Cognitive automation affects people with college degrees, professional licenses, and office jobs.

It will not replace everyone overnight. But it will compress wages, reduce hiring, and shift the remaining work toward "AI oversight" roles. The people who used to do the analysis will now be checking the AI's analysis.

Most policy plans assume displaced workers can just become caregivers or construction workers. That is not realistic. A 45-year-old accountant cannot easily become a nurse. The skills, pay, and geography do not match.

We need a plan that matches displaced workers to new roles that actually use their skills—not a plan that pretends everyone can start over.

## Part 5: What We Can Actually Do—A Realistic Roadmap

This is the part where most documents either give up or get utopian. They say "vote harder" or they propose a perfect system that has no chance of passing.

This document tries something different. It treats America like a house that is still being lived in while it is being repaired. You cannot tear it down and rebuild it. You have to fix the wiring while the lights are on.

### The Principles

1. **Start with what helps people immediately.** Do not wait for the perfect law. Do what reduces pain now.
2. **Harden against court vetoes.** Write laws so clearly that judges cannot strike them down on technicalities.
3. **Use states as testing grounds.** Let successful state programs become the national model, rather than forcing one-size-fits-all from Washington.
4. **Match workers to new roles, not just "any" roles.** Respect people's skills and experience.
5. **Pay for it without bankrupting the country.** Use assets the government already owns and close tax loopholes before raising rates on ordinary people.
6. **Build in feedback loops.** Every program gets evaluated after five years. If it does not work, it ends.

### Phase 1: Stop the Bleeding (Years 1–2)

**Housing:** Give federal incentives to states that allow apartment buildings near transit and jobs. Release surplus federal land for affordable housing. This does not require Congress to pass a massive bill—it can be done through existing housing programs.

**Healthcare:** Expand Medicare's power to negotiate drug prices. Enforce the hospital price transparency rules that already exist. Let states experiment with "all-payer rate setting" (one price for everyone, like Maryland already does).

**Work:** Launch retraining programs for AI oversight roles—checking AI outputs for bias and errors. These are 6–12 month certifications, not four-year degrees. Offer wage insurance: if you take a lower-paying transition job, the government covers half the pay cut for two years.

**Competition:** Strengthen antitrust enforcement. Block mergers that give one company 30% or more of a market. Let citizens and states sue monopolists directly, with the monopolist paying legal fees if they lose.

**Fiscal:** Close the "stepped-up basis" loophole (which lets wealthy heirs avoid capital gains taxes entirely). Cap the mortgage interest deduction so it stops subsidizing mansions. Tax hedge fund managers' income as ordinary income, not capital gains.

### Phase 2: Fix the Institutions (Years 3–5)

**Elections:** Let states adopt ranked-choice voting and independent redistricting commissions. Give federal matching funds to states that do this, so there is a financial incentive.

**Civil Service:** Reduce the number of political appointees in federal agencies by 30%. Restore merit-based hiring for senior officials. The government works better when the people running it know what they are doing.

**Automatic Stabilizers:** Create a pre-authorized fund that automatically deploys money for infrastructure and relief when unemployment hits a trigger level. No more waiting for Congress to argue while the economy burns.

**Governance AI:** Use artificial intelligence to reduce government waste—processing benefits faster, detecting fraud, and analyzing procurement bids for collusion. But mandate that all government AI be open-source and auditable. No black boxes making decisions about people's rights.

### Phase 3: Build the New System (Years 6–10)

**Healthcare:** If Congress has not passed comprehensive reform by Year 8, automatically adopt the most successful state model as the federal baseline. States can opt out, but they have to do so actively.

**AI and Labor:** Create a "Productivity Dividend Trust Fund" that captures a portion of AI-generated productivity gains and distributes them as public investment. Treat advanced computing power like Alaska treats oil: a shared resource that belongs to everyone.

**Housing:** Expand social housing using revenue from leasing federal land. Not housing projects—mixed-income, well-designed communities like Vienna has built for a century.

**Politics:** Scale nationally the electoral reforms that worked at the state level. Public financing for campaigns so candidates do not have to spend half their time fundraising from the wealthy.

## Part 6: What You Can Do Right Now

You are one person. You cannot pass a law. But you are not powerless. Here is what actually works.

**1. Talk about it.**

Not in an abstract, political way. In a personal way. "My rent went up $300 this year and my paycheck didn't. I read that this is not an accident—it's a structural change that started in the 1970s." When you connect your own experience to the bigger picture, other people recognize their own experience.

**2. Ask questions at local meetings.**

City council, school board, county commission. Ask: "Are we treating symptoms or causes?" If they say they are building affordable housing, ask how many units and who qualifies. If they say they are bringing jobs, ask what the wages are. Be polite, but be specific.

**3. Support state-level experiments.**

National politics is gridlocked. State politics is not. If your state is considering ranked-choice voting, zoning reform, or a public healthcare option, show up. Write a letter. Testify for two minutes. State laws become national models.

**4. Vote in primaries.**

Most people vote in general elections, where the outcome is often predetermined. Primaries are where the real choice happens. And they are usually decided by a tiny fraction of voters. Your vote counts more in a primary than in a general election.

**5. Find one other person.**

Movements do not start with crowds. They start with two people who keep showing up. Find someone who read this and cares. Meet every two weeks. Each of you does one thing: one post, one letter, one conversation, one question at a meeting.

**6. Do not let perfect be the enemy of good.**

You will disagree with some of the solutions in this document. That is fine. You do not have to defend every line. You just have to agree that the diagnosis is real: the system changed, and it stopped working for ordinary people.

## Conclusion: The System Can Change Because It Has Changed Before

The United States has been here before.

In the Gilded Age of the 1890s, wealth was concentrated at the top, workers had no power, and politics was corrupt. Ordinary people organized, voted, and demanded change. The result was the Progressive Era: antitrust laws, direct election of senators, labor protections, and the income tax.

In the Great Depression of the 1930s, the economy collapsed and a quarter of the country was unemployed. The New Deal rebuilt the social contract: Social Security, the minimum wage, the 40-hour work week, bank regulation, and massive public investment.

Both of those transformations were led by regular people who decided that the current arrangement was not good enough. They were not experts. They were citizens who paid attention and refused to accept the excuse that "this is just how things are."

The same is possible now. The system is not broken beyond repair. It is a live system that can be patched, redirected, and gradually rebuilt.

But it will not happen on its own. It will happen because people like you read the facts, connect them to their own lives, and decide that the current direction is not acceptable.

You are not failing. The math changed. And math can be changed back.
